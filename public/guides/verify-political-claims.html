How to verify political claims online

A practical, nonpartisan way to investigate screenshots, speeches, election updates and policy announcements before sharing them.

PoliticsHub News Desk · Explainer · Published 9 November 2026

Start with the exact claim

Write down what the post actually asserts, including the date, location, institution and people mentioned. A screenshot may omit a question, a qualification or an earlier sentence. Distinguish a claim that an official made a statement from the separate question of whether the statement itself is supported by evidence. For videos, try to find the original recording and enough context to understand what was being discussed.

A question such as “Has a law passed?” is different from “Has a minister proposed a law?” or “Has a new rule taken effect?” The wording tells you which public record to seek. Be wary of adding conclusions or motivations that the original evidence does not establish.

Identify the earliest attributable source

Find the original press release, full speech, court document, election notice, parliamentary record or on-the-record interview where possible. Compare its wording with the viral summary. A social post quoting another post is not independent corroboration. When a newspaper cites an agency, the agency's report and the underlying primary record are useful additional reference points.

Check the author's identity, publication date and any corrections attached to the source. If the original is inaccessible, be explicit about the limitation rather than describing an indirect quote as personally verified. Translations should preserve important qualifiers such as “may,” “proposed,” “alleged” and “subject to approval.”

Check dates, jurisdiction and status

Old images often return during new events. Search for the earliest appearance of a quotation or photograph, and verify whether it concerns the same district, election or government. A national headline can obscure the fact that a measure applies only to one state, department or category. The responsible authority and the relevant date matter.

For a government action, distinguish consultation papers, draft rules, cabinet decisions, Gazette notifications and requirements currently in force. For a court matter, separate arguments, interim orders and final judgments. For an election, distinguish opinion surveys, live leads and official results. Each represents a different kind of evidence.

Compare independent reporting without treating repetition as proof

Several articles may quote the same original press note. Count their underlying sources rather than the number of websites repeating a claim. Seek direct documentation and coverage that explains its limitations. If sources appear inconsistent, compare timestamps and definitions; a preliminary figure and a revised figure need not describe the same snapshot.

When a dispute remains unresolved, label the uncertainty instead of choosing an unsupported interpretation. A useful explanation separates verified facts, attributed allegations, response statements and questions that available documents do not answer.

Use a five-point share checklist

Before forwarding a claim ask: Who originally made it? When was it recorded? Where does it apply? What official document or full recording supports it? And what remains unconfirmed? Include the original link and a date whenever an event is still unfolding. A cropped image without source context should not be treated as conclusive evidence.
